Greek Museum



The Greco-Roman Museum is the oldest building in Egypt, specifically designed to preserve and display antiquities. It is the only museum specializing in displaying the antiquities and civilization of Egypt during the Greek and Roman eras.



Since 1889, the Italian scholar Giuseppe Botti sought to establish a museum in Alexandria to house its collection of archaeological discoveries. He began in a modest rented property, which was inaugurated by Khedive Abbas Hilmi II on October 17, 1892. From its inception, the museum fostered a cultural revival, leading to an increase in discoveries and creating a pressing need for a new, more spacious building. On September 12, 1894, Nubar Pasha laid the foundation stone for a new museum designed by architects Dietrich and Steinon in a classical style. Khedive Abbas Hilmi II inaugurated it again on September 26, 1895. Several development projects for the museum have been undertaken. In 1982, a new wing was added connecting the western and eastern wings. The current design for the museum building was finalized in 2015, and in 2018, work resumed with the addition of an upper floor.

The museum's current collection comprises approximately 6,000 artifacts spanning from the pre-Alexander era to the Byzantine period. The museum building consists of a ground floor with 27 galleries, in addition to restoration laboratories and storage facilities. An upper floor houses four galleries: the Museum Education Hall, the Archives and Records Hall, the Gypsum Gallery, and the Study Hall. Another floor displays a number of artifacts in several galleries, organized by type. Examples of these include: the statue of Emperor Diocletian, a rare red statue representing the emperor who persecuted Christians, leading to his reign being known as the Age of Martyrs; and the Apis Bull statue, a stone statue displayed near the Crocodile Temple, depicting the god Osiris in the form of the Apis bull, with the sun disk and the sacred cobra between its horns. It was discovered in 1895 at the Serapeum by the archaeologist Botti, the museum's founder. The Queen Berenice Mosaic Panel With Protruding Eyes One of the museum's most unusual exhibits, this mosaic panel realistically depicts Queen Berenice, wife of Ptolemy II or III, with protruding eyes due to a thyroid condition. The panel was discovered in the Raml Station area, and its restoration and assembly took many months. The panel showcases the art of mosaic with its natural colors. The Isis Varia Statue The largest of the museum's submerged artifacts, this colossal statue of the goddess Isis was recovered from the Eastern Harbor in 1962 and is now displayed in the museum's inner courtyard, becoming a popular spot for taking souvenir photos.
